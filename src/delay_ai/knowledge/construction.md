# Construction context (UK)

General knowledge about how UK buildings are built. It goes into the planner's
and the chatbot's prompts. Keep it factual and edit it with the client's own
experience.

## Typical sequence for a framed residential block
1. Enabling works and site set-up: hoarding, welfare, temporary services.
2. Substructure: piling, pile caps, ground beams, ground floor slab.
3. Frame: steel or concrete frame, then metal decking and floor slabs.
4. Envelope: roof structure and covering, curtain walling or cladding, windows.
5. **Watertight**: the milestone that lets internal trades start.
6. Internal works: MEP first fix, drylining and partitions, MEP second fix,
   kitchens and bathrooms, decoration and flooring.
7. Lifts: installation follows the shaft and the frame; commissioning needs
   permanent power.
8. External works and landscaping: usually have float.
9. Testing and commissioning, snagging, then **practical completion**.

## Terms
- **Programme**: the construction schedule.
- **First fix / second fix**: services and carpentry before and after walls are
  boarded.
- **Watertight**: roof and envelope closed, so internal finishes are protected.
- **Practical completion (PC)**: the building is handed over; usually the
  contractual completion date, with liquidated damages for each week late.
- **Long-lead items**: fabricated or bespoke packages (structural steel,
  curtain walling, lifts) with lead times of months and little flexibility.

## Materials and lead times
- Commodity items (plasterboard, rebar, blocks, sanitaryware, flooring) are
  usually available from several merchants within days or weeks.
- Fabricated steel depends on mill rolling slots, fabrication and galvanising;
  slips of one to three weeks are common.
- Unitised curtain walling and lifts are bespoke; a late supplier is hard to
  replace mid-project.
- Kitchens are usually delivered in one or more drops; phasing by floor is common.

## Weather
- Tower and mobile cranes stop lifting in high wind; gusts above roughly 38 mph
  (about 17 m/s) usually halt lifts, and lower limits apply to large panels such
  as curtain walling or decking sheets.
- Concrete pours need protection or postponement in frost (below about 5 °C and
  falling); screeds and plaster dry slowly in cold, damp weather.
- Roofing, membranes and external sealants need dry conditions; heavy rain stops
  them, and wet weather slows groundworks and external works.
- Once the building is watertight, internal trades are largely unaffected by
  weather.
- A day of bad weather during a task usually means a day added to it, not a
  shift of the whole programme.

## Labour and plant
- A gang short of operatives works proportionally slower: two out of six fitters
  missing is roughly a third less output.
- Specialist crews (steel erectors, curtain wall installers, lift engineers) are
  hard to replace at short notice; general trades are easier.
- Plant breakdowns (crane, hoist) stop the tasks that depend on them until a
  replacement arrives, often a few days.
- Holidays (Christmas, Easter, summer weeks) reduce labour availability.

## Safety and regulation
- CDM 2015 places duties on the principal contractor for planning and managing
  work safely. Programme changes must not compromise temporary works, edge
  protection or safe access.
- Working at height, lifting operations (LOLER) and temporary works need
  sign-off before sequencing changes are made on site.
- Weekend and night working may need local authority consent and neighbour
  notification.

## Commercial
- Acceleration (extra gangs, overtime, weekend work) costs money and is usually
  a variation or the contractor's own risk, depending on who caused the delay.
- A supplier's late delivery may be recoverable from the supplier under the
  order terms; keep the email record.
